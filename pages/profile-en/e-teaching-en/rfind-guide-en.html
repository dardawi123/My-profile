User Guide: Research Findings and Discussion

This tool takes you from organised research evidence to a well-written findings section and a coherent, critical discussion. Module A (Steps 1–8) organises, structures, visualises and writes your findings. Module B (Steps 9–17) interprets them, connects them with theory and previous studies, and builds the discussion. Steps 18–19 evaluate the work and produce a final report.

Overview

Introduction to the platform: the Research Findings and Discussion Builder is an interactive research-writing and interpretation laboratory, not an information page. Each step explains a principle, shows a film or television example (often with weaker and stronger wording), collects your entries and checks them with transparent rules. Your entries feed one findings draft, one discussion draft and one report.

Academic purpose and intended users: postgraduate students, doctoral researchers and academics in the Cinema and Television track of the Department of Audio-Visual Production who study films, series, drama, audiovisual texts, audiences, representation and screen media. The tool prioritises qualitative and mixed-methods research (thematic analysis, qualitative content analysis, interviews, focus groups, narrative, discourse, semiotic and textual analysis, feminist film criticism and reception studies) and also supports surveys and quantitative content analysis. It does not impose one reporting structure: thematic findings, semiotic readings, statistical results and mixed-methods integration are reported differently.

The two modules:

  • Module A — Research Findings: Organisation, Writing and Presentation (Steps 1–8): research context, presentation approach, evidence matrix, key findings and themes, question–findings alignment, supporting evidence, visualisation and the findings draft.
  • Module B — Research Discussion: Interpretation and Scholarly Synthesis (Steps 9–17): discussion plan, interpretation, theory–findings integration, comparison with previous studies, unexpected findings, implications, limitations, future research and the discussion draft.
  • Refinement and report (Steps 18–19): fourteen-criterion evaluation and the final report.

All matrices are shared between the Builder and the website sections: what you enter in one place appears in the other. Every matrix can be exported and imported as CSV or JSON.

Have ready before you start: your research questions, your analysed data (coded transcripts, scene notes, statistical output), the previous studies you will discuss (with full references) and your target journal’s or university’s requirements. The tool uses only what you enter: it never invents findings, quotations, statistics, interpretations or references.

Moving around: use Next step and Previous step, or the numbered circles grouped by module. Fields marked with an asterisk (*) are required; you cannot continue until they are complete. Everything saves automatically on this device.

Example used in this guide: a qualitative semiotic study of how two Saudi streaming series construct belonging through the family home, with a combined Results and Discussion section.

Open the tool from the Teaching Experience page (Digital Learning Resources) or directly at: Research Findings: Writing, Presentation, and Discussion in Film and Television Studies. Every Builder step also has a “Guidance for this step” link that opens this guide at the same step in a new tab.

Learning outcomes

After completing the tool’s steps you will be able to:

  • Organise research evidence in a traceable matrix that links every finding to coded, attributed evidence.
  • Choose a findings structure that fits the methodological tradition and the requirements of the venue.
  • Write clear, evidence-supported findings and align them honestly with the research questions.
  • Visualise findings with transparent calculations, without confusing frequency with importance.
  • Interpret findings, connect them with the theoretical framework and compare them critically with previous studies.
  • Write a coherent discussion with credible implications, limitations and future directions, and self-assess it.

The report contains the research title and profile, research questions, presentation plan, key findings, themes, supporting and audiovisual evidence, question–findings alignment, charts and thematic map, joint display, findings draft, discussion outline, interpretation, theoretical interpretation, literature comparison, unexpected findings, implications, limitations, future research, discussion draft, evaluation results, suggested improvements, final texts and references.

  • One item, one row: a quotation, a scene or a statistic is one item of evidence with its own code.
  • Exact and attributed: copy quotations exactly and attribute them with participant codes; describe scenes before interpreting them and give timestamps.
  • Traceable: link every item to the finding it supports; aim for at least two items per finding from different sources.
  • Negative cases: record evidence that does not fit and report it.
  • Spreadsheets: export any matrix as CSV, edit it in Excel (open as UTF-8) and import it back. Findings appear as F1, F2… and questions as MRQ, RQ1… so the links survive.
  • Introduce, show, explain, connect: state the finding, present the evidence, explain what it shows and relate it to a research question.
  • Choose the right place for interpretation: in a separate Results section, describe; interpretation and literature belong in the Discussion. In a combined section, interpretation follows each finding.
  • Quotations: introduce each extract; set extracts of 40 words or more as block quotations (APA 7).
  • Statistics: report test statistic, degrees of freedom, exact p value, effect size and confidence interval.
  • Tables and figures: number and title them, refer to them in the text and do not repeat every value.
  • Tense: past for what you did and found; present for what tables show and for interpretation (see the tense guide in Findings Writing).
  • Discuss, do not repeat: open with a brief synthesis, then interpret, compare and theorise. The checker flags sentences copied from the findings draft.
  • Ground interpretations: every interpretation needs evidence; consider alternatives and explain why your reading is more convincing.
  • Hedge appropriately: “suggests”, “may”, “appears” match claims to evidence; avoid “proves”.
  • Return to the questions: say what the findings mean for each research question, including questions only partly answered.
  • No new findings: everything discussed must appear in the findings first.

For each key finding, name the concept and assumption, the evidence and the relationship. Findings may support, extend or complicate a theory, reveal its limitations or suggest new relationships. If every finding “supports” the theory, ask whether you are forcing confirmation. The Theory–Findings section explains the five relationships.

Compare findings with specific studies you have read, and explain agreement and difference by context, sample, period or method. Never claim agreement or novelty without evidence; write “among the studies reviewed”. Each comparison needs a full reference. Use the phrase bank in the Literature Comparison section.

  • Colours and statuses: “Good” (green) needs no work, “Revise” (amber) is worth a look, “Missing” (red) is an essential element that is absent, and “Note” (blue) is information to consider.
  • Areas: each check names its area (for example “Question alignment” or “Previous studies”) so you know which step to revisit.
  • Suggested scores: come from the checks in the related areas; accept or change them. Recommendations appear for criteria rated 3 or below.
  • Overall level: “Ready for supervisor review” (4.5+), “Strong draft”, “Developing” or “Early draft”.

Reading the feedback

  • Colours and statuses: “Good” (green) needs no work, “Revise” (amber) is worth a look, “Missing” (red) is an essential element that is absent, and “Note” (blue) is information to consider.
  • “Fits your technique” badge: marks the usual presentation structure for your analytical technique; it does not decide for you.
  • Evidence checks: flag quotations without participant codes, scenes without timestamps and findings supported by fewer than two items.
  • Alignment checks: flag questions with no linked findings and remind you never to fabricate findings to fill them.
  • Draft checks: flag interpretation in a separate Results section, repetition of results in the discussion, overclaiming and unsupported claims.
  • Suggested scores: appear under each criterion in Step 18 and come from the checks; accept or change them. Recommendations appear for criteria rated 3 or below.

Saving, editing, copying, printing and exporting

  • Automatic saving: every change is saved in this browser on this device; the time of the last save appears above the steps.
  • Editing: return to any step through the numbered circles. Matrices are shared with the website sections.
  • Copying: use Copy next to each draft, “Copy findings”, “Copy discussion” or “Copy report”.
  • Printing and PDF: in Step 19 click “Print / Save as PDF” and choose “Save as PDF”. Only the report is printed, with charts and the thematic map.
  • Downloading: “Download Word (.doc)”, “Download text (.txt)”, “Export all matrices (CSV)”; each matrix and visual can also be exported separately.
  • Project files: “Export my project (JSON)” saves everything; “Import project” restores it on another device or browser.
  • Switching language: your entries and current step are kept; your own text is never translated.
  • Deleting: “Delete my saved data” (click twice to confirm) removes your project from this browser. Do this on shared computers.

Troubleshooting

  • “Next step” does nothing: a required field or matrix is incomplete; the message at the top and the highlighted fields show what is missing.
  • A finding does not appear in a list: it has no statement or label yet; lists update a moment after you type.
  • The draft did not change after I edited an earlier step: you edited the draft directly. Use “Rebuild from my entries” (your edits will be replaced) or edit the draft yourself.
  • A discussion part is empty: the related step has no complete rows, or the part is switched off.
  • The chart is empty: enter labels and numerical values; Arabic-Indic and Western digits are both accepted.
  • The thematic map is empty: add themes in the Qualitative Themes Builder (Step 4).
  • The CSV import failed: the first row must contain the column names exported by the tool; save the file as CSV (UTF-8).
  • Arabic text looks wrong in Excel: open the CSV through Data › From Text/CSV and choose UTF-8.
  • My data disappeared: data are stored per browser and device; private windows and clearing site data remove them. Import your exported JSON file.
  • The printed report includes other parts of the page: print from Step 19 using the button, not the browser menu.

Academic integrity and research ethics

  • Your content, your responsibility: every finding, quotation, statistic, interpretation and citation comes from you. You remain responsible for its accuracy.
  • No invented content: the tool never generates data, findings, quotations, statistics, interpretations or references. Illustrative examples are labelled; published examples link to verified DOIs.
  • Participants: use codes or pseudonyms, protect identities and report only what consent covers.
  • Audiovisual material: cite titles, episodes and timestamps; use stills sparingly within fair use.
  • Rule-based, not AI: checks rely on word lists, counts, citation patterns and links between your entries. They can miss problems or flag acceptable wording and do not judge scholarly quality.
  • Not a substitute: the tool supports learning and refinement. It does not replace expert judgement, supervision or peer review.

Frequently asked questions

Why can’t I move to the next step?
Some required fields (marked with an asterisk) are empty or too short. A message appears at the top of the step and each missing field is highlighted with a note below it; complete them and click Next again.
Where are my answers saved?
In this browser’s local storage on this device only; nothing is sent to a server. On another device or browser your answers appear only after you import a JSON file you exported earlier.
Does the tool write or interpret my findings for me?
No. Templates only arrange your own entries and the checks are rule-based prompts; no findings, quotations, statistics, interpretations or references are added that you did not enter.
Should I separate or combine Results and Discussion?
It depends on the journal or university and the methodological tradition. Choose Option A or B in Step 2; the checks adapt to your choice.
What do I do with a research question my data do not answer?
Mark it “Not addressed by the evidence” in Step 5 and explain why in the note; this appears in the discussion. Never fabricate a finding to fill it.
Does the frequency of a theme show its importance?
No. In qualitative research frequency shows presence, not analytical importance; the “qualitative counts” option in Step 7 adds a caption saying so.
How do I move the Evidence Matrix into Excel?
Use “Export CSV” in the matrix and open the file in Excel as UTF-8. Findings appear as F1, F2… so the links survive when you use “Import CSV or JSON”.
How do I save the report as a PDF?
In the final step click the print button (“Print” or “Print / save as PDF”), then choose “Save as PDF” in your browser’s print dialog. Only the report is printed, not the rest of the site.
Are my answers translated when I switch to the Arabic version?
No. Your answers move to the other version exactly as you wrote them; only the interface and guidance change language.
My answers have disappeared. What happened?
Usually the browser data was cleared, private (incognito) browsing was used, or the tool was opened in a different browser. They can only be restored from an exported JSON file, so export a backup regularly.
Does the tool use artificial intelligence?
No. All feedback, formulations and suggested scores come from transparent programmed rules (word lists and templates), so the tool may miss a problem or flag something that is fine; your judgement and your supervisor’s come first.
