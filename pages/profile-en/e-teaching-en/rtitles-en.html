    Research Alignment Matrix

    Alignment checks

    The checks look for shared key concepts and compatible wording. They point to places worth revising; they do not judge conceptual quality. Every element of a study should align: the title creates an expectation that the questions, methods, results and conclusion must meet (attached tutorials; Creswell & Creswell, 2023).

    Methodological Guide

    Formulating research titles in Film and Television Studies

    Evidence-based recommendations are tagged and referenced; illustrative examples were written for teaching and are labelled as such.

    Evidence-based recommendation

    Characteristics of a strong research title

    • Accurate and not misleading: it reflects what the study actually did (Tullu, 2019).
    • Concise and precise: descriptive, direct and specific, without whimsical words or non-standard abbreviations (Tullu, 2019).
    • Focused, with key terms: APA sets no maximum length but asks that titles stay focused and include key terms (American Psychological Association, n.d.).
    • Searchable: the title carries the words researchers type into databases (attached tutorials; Tullu, 2019).
    • Grammatical: keep the articles and prepositions that make the phrase read naturally (attached tutorials).
    • Clear, accurate and suitably specialised, neither too long nor too short, and linked to the research problem (Guendouz, 2023).
    Evidence-based recommendation

    Titles differ across disciplines

    • Title patterns differ substantially between disciplines, reflecting their characteristics and topics (Hyland & Zou, 2022).
    • Over 60 years, titles have grown longer and more often take question or compound forms; methods appear more in hard-science titles and results more in softer fields (Jiang & Hyland, 2023).
    • In one corpus, humanities titles averaged about 9.8 words against about 12.2 in the natural sciences; compound titles made up about 58% of humanities titles, and question titles were more frequent there (Yang et al., 2025).
    • Colons are used more in the arts than in the sciences; readers generally preferred colon titles, and colons did not affect later citations (Hartley, 2007).
    • Nominal-group titles are the most frequent construction; full-sentence titles are almost absent in the social sciences (Soler, 2007).
    • Title conventions also vary with authors’ cultural and linguistic backgrounds (Xie, 2020).
    Evidence-based recommendation

    Length and discoverability: mixed evidence

    • Journals publishing papers with shorter titles received more citations per paper, a correlational finding (Letchford et al., 2015).
    • In management science, title length showed no clear relationship with citations; balanced structure had a modest effect (Nair & Gibbert, 2016).
    • The attached tutorials recommend roughly 10–16 words and placing key terms at the beginning and end.
    • Treat concision as a matter of readability and accuracy, not as a guarantee of citations.
    Evidence-based recommendation

    Titles in qualitative and mixed-methods studies

    • Qualitative studies explore a central phenomenon in its context; the title should name that phenomenon and avoid wording that implies cause and effect (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Creswell & Creswell, 2023).
    • Interpretive wording — “readings of”, “experiences of”, “representation of”, “negotiating” — signals the kind of knowledge a qualitative study produces.
    • Mixed-methods titles may name the design when integration of both strands is central to the contribution (Creswell & Creswell, 2023).
    • Quantitative titles name variables and the population; effect wording requires a design able to test it.

    Theory and method terminology in titles

    • Name a theory when it defines your contribution, e.g. “… : A Uses and Gratification Approach” (published example in the attached file).
    • Name the design when it helps readers judge the study, e.g. “… : A Qualitative Study” or “… : A Case Study of …” (published examples).
    • Avoid stacking several method terms; one design term is usually enough.
    • Refining a title requires knowing earlier studies, to avoid repeating existing titles and to make the contribution visible (Cherabcha & Boufrida, 2022).

    Common weaknesses in Film and Television Studies titles

    • Overly broad wording (“Women in Arab Media”).
    • Ambiguous concepts (“the role of”, “the impact of media on society”).
    • Redundant expressions (“differences and similarities”, “problems … and its problems”).
    • Excessive length that packs the abstract into the title.
    • Unnecessary methodological terminology.
    • Inappropriate causal or evaluative claims (“negative portrayal”, “harmful effects”) in interpretive studies.
    • Titles that do not reflect the proposed research or the actual corpus.

    About the Tool

    About this tool

    Purpose

    This platform helps researchers and postgraduate students in the Cinema and Television track of the Department of Audio-Visual Production to develop, evaluate, refine and formulate academically appropriate research titles, with priority given to qualitative and mixed-methods research.

    How it works

    All feedback, title formulations and suggested scores come from transparent, rule-based JavaScript (word lists, patterns and templates that can be read in the source files). The tool does not use artificial intelligence, does not verify claims against the literature and does not search academic databases.

    Privacy

    Your answers are saved in this browser’s local storage only, so that you can return to them later. Nothing is sent to a server. You can export, import or delete your data at any time.

    Academic disclaimer. This website provides educational and methodological assistance. It does not replace academic supervision, scholarly judgement or research verification. Self-assessment scores are tools for reflection, not validated measures of quality.
