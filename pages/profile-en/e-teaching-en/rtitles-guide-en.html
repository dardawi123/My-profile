User Guide: Refining and Formulating Research Titles

This tool takes you in eight steps from a broad idea to a clear, precise research title, then evaluates it, compares it with alternatives and produces an editable report. This guide explains every field, what to enter, and a correct example for each.

Overview

Who it is for: postgraduate students and researchers in Film and Television Studies, with priority given to qualitative and mixed-methods research.

What you get at the end:

  • An initial title, a refined title and a selected final title.
  • A self-assessment of the title on ten criteria, scored 1 to 5.
  • A comparison of two or three alternative titles.
  • A final report you can edit, copy, print or download as Word or text.

Have ready before you start:

  • A broad idea of your topic and its subfield.
  • The material you will study (films, series or an audience), its context and its period.
  • A first wording of your research problem or aim in one or two sentences.
  • Your research questions and theoretical framework, if you have them (optional).

Moving around: use Next and Previous at the bottom of each step, or click a step number in the bar at the top. Fields marked with an asterisk (*) are required; you cannot move to the next step until they are filled. Your answers save automatically in your browser.

Example used in this guide: a researcher studying intergenerational conflict in Saudi Ramadan series, 2019–2025, using a qualitative approach.

Accessing the tool

Open the tool from the Teaching Experience page (Digital Learning Resources) or directly at: Refining and Formulating Research Titles. Every Builder step also has a “Guidance for this step” link that opens this guide at the same step in a new tab.

Learning outcomes

After completing the tool’s steps you will be able to:

  • Turn a broad interest into a defined topic with its phenomenon, context and material.
  • Make a justified decision on whether to name a theory or method in the title.
  • Draft an initial title and refine it against ten quality dimensions.
  • Self-assess the title, compare alternatives and select a justified final title.

Research Title Builder: the eight steps

Step 1 — Identify the Research Topic

Goal: move from a general interest to a defined topic with its phenomenon, context and material.

Field What to enter Correct example
General research topic * Your broad area of interest, as a short phrase Family in Gulf television drama
Academic field or specialisation * Choose the closest subfield from the list Drama and Audiovisual Narrative
Research phenomenon or issue * A noun phrase (not a sentence or question); it becomes the core of your title intergenerational conflict
Research context * Place, culture or platform; start with a preposition when “in” does not fit Saudi Arabia — or: on streaming platforms
Audiovisual material or population * What you will analyse (works) or whom you will study (people) Saudi Ramadan series — or: young female viewers
Timeframe (optional) The period the study covers 2019–2025
Proposed methodological approach * Choose Qualitative, Mixed methods or Quantitative Qualitative

Output: a clearly defined, narrowed topic, shown at the bottom of the step.

Step 2 — Clarify the Research Focus

Goal: decide exactly what you will investigate, which concepts matter most, and the study’s boundaries. A title is a contract with readers: it promises exactly what the study delivers.

Field What to enter Correct example
What exactly will the study investigate? * The analytical focus in one sentence How intergenerational conflict is narrated and resolved
Key concepts or analytical dimensions * Concepts separated by commas, most important first (the first is used in generated titles) authority, generational change, family honour
What is central to the research? * Choose: a representation, narrative or form, an audience experience, a production or professional practice, or an industry, platform or policy Narrative or form
Geographical, cultural or temporal scope (optional) The boundaries of your corpus Saudi productions, 2019–2025
Comparison units (optional) Only for comparative studies; otherwise leave empty Saudi and Egyptian Ramadan series
Aspects to include / exclude (optional) What is explicitly in or out Include: social drama — Exclude: comedy series
Research problem or aim (brief) * One or two sentences; used to check the title matches the problem Little is known about how streaming-era Saudi series construct intergenerational relations.
Research questions (optional) One question per line; shown in the Research Alignment Matrix How do selected Saudi Ramadan series narrate intergenerational conflict?
What is distinctive about this study? (optional) Your expected contribution, briefly First focus on the streaming-era corpus

Step 3 — Theoretical and Methodological Orientation

Goal: decide deliberately whether the title names a theory or a method. Neither is required: naming them helps when the theory defines the contribution or the design tells readers how to judge the study.

Field What to enter Correct example
Methodological orientation * Choose the closest analytical approach, e.g. semiotic analysis, narrative analysis, audience reception study, case study, quantitative content analysis Narrative analysis
Design details (optional) Data source, sample and analysis Narrative analysis of 6 purposively selected series
Should the title name the method? * Name it / Keep it out / Not decided yet Name it (it tells readers the kind of analysis)
Theoretical perspective (optional) The theory, or leave empty if your design is inductive Narratology
Should the title name the theory? * The same three options Keep it out (it belongs in the abstract and keywords)

Step 4 — Generate the Initial Title

  1. Keywords: generated from Steps 1–3. Click the words that must appear in the title (e.g. intergenerational conflict, Saudi Ramadan series).
  2. Title structure: choose one of six: descriptive, analytical, comparative, theory-oriented, methodology-oriented, or main title and subtitle.
  3. Short main title (optional): only for the main-title–subtitle structure, e.g. “Negotiating Tradition”.
  4. Generated formulations: click one to copy it into the next field, then always edit it; these are rule-based templates.
  5. Initial working title *: correct example: “Intergenerational Conflict in Saudi Ramadan Series (2019–2025): A Narrative Analysis”.
  6. Save as alternative: save any other wording you like, to compare in Step 7.

Step 5 — Research Title Refinement

The tool checks your initial title on ten dimensions: clarity, precision, specificity, conciseness, academic terminology, research scope, theoretical relevance, methodological alignment, readability, and relevance to Film and Television Studies.

  1. Read the feedback: green means no issue, amber means the dimension needs review.
  2. The tool offers two ready versions: a cleaned version (filler, promotional words and the final full stop removed) and a template version for your chosen structure. Click “Use this version” to copy one.
  3. Write your **Refined research title *** and watch the counter “Dimensions still needing review” fall towards zero.
  4. Open “Show the analysis of my refined title” to see the remaining comments.
Before (weak) After (better) What changed
The Role of Drama in Society: A Comprehensive and Innovative Study Intergenerational Conflict in Saudi Ramadan Series (2019–2025): A Narrative Analysis Vague “role” and promotional words removed; material, context and period added
The Negative Impact of Turkish Series on Women Saudi Women’s Readings of Turkish Series: A Qualitative Study Causal and evaluative claim unsuited to qualitative research removed; population named

Step 6 — Research Title Quality Evaluation

Rate the title from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on ten criteria: clarity and readability; focus and specificity; conciseness; accuracy of academic terminology; alignment with the research problem; theoretical appropriateness; methodological consistency; relevance to the discipline; feasibility and appropriate scope; potential scholarly significance.

  • Click “Apply suggested scores” to start from the tool’s suggestion, then adjust any score to your own judgement.
  • Any criterion you rate 3 or below gets an improvement recommendation in the result panel.
  • This is an educational self-assessment and does not replace your supervisor’s review.

Step 7 — Compare Alternative Titles

Field What to enter Correct example
Title A * Your refined title (filled automatically) Intergenerational Conflict in Saudi Ramadan Series (2019–2025): A Narrative Analysis
Title B * An alternative with a different structure Negotiating Authority: Narrating Intergenerational Conflict in Saudi Ramadan Drama
Title C (optional) A third alternative, if any Generational Relations in Streaming-Era Saudi Ramadan Series
Selected refined title * Your final wording; used in the report and the tools Title A, with a final edit

Click “Reload from my initial, refined and saved titles” to fill in your saved titles. The tool shows how each title performs on the ten dimensions; the final choice is yours and your supervisor’s.

Reading the feedback

  • Colours: in the analysis, green means a dimension needs no review and amber means it needs review, with a short reason.
  • Counter: “Dimensions still needing review” in Step 5 falls as you address each comment.
  • Suggested scores: in Step 6 each criterion shows a score suggested by the automatic checks; accept it or change it to your own judgement.
  • Checklist (Tool A): “Detected” means the wording rules found it; “Check manually” needs your judgement.

Other sections and supporting tools

These tools read your Builder answers; some can also be used on their own with any title.

Section (site menu) What it does How to use it
Title Refinement (Tool B) Compares an initial and a refined title criterion by criterion and explains “what changed and why” Click “Load from the Title Builder”, or type both titles
Title Quality Evaluation (Tool A) A 15-item checklist, e.g. names the material, no filler, no causal claims, searchable keywords Paste a title, then tick each item once addressed. “Detected” = found by the tool; “Check manually” = needs your judgement
Research Alignment Matrix (Tool C) Shows how the title connects to the problem, questions, theory and method Updates automatically; review empty cells and warnings
Academic Examples (Tool D) Weak and improved teaching titles, and published titles analysed for structure Filter by area
Methodological Guide Features of a strong title, title types, common weaknesses, a bilingual glossary Read before starting or when unsure; search the glossary in English or Arabic
Academic References The guide’s sources in APA 7 Cite them in your methods summary

Summary formula: core topic + key method or lens + context or contribution − unnecessary words. Not every title needs all four parts.

Saving, exporting and privacy

  • Automatic saving: your answers are saved in this browser only; nothing is sent to a server. A line at the top of the tool shows when it last saved.
  • Switching language: the العربية button at the top opens the Arabic version with the same answers (your text is not translated).
  • Working on another device: click “Export my data (JSON)”, move the file, then click “Import data” on the other device. Also export a copy before clearing your browser data.
  • Deleting: “Delete my saved data” asks for a second click to confirm and cannot be undone unless you import an exported copy.
  • Private or incognito browsing: your answers may be erased when the window closes, so export them first.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Mistake Example Instead
Too broad Women in Arab Media Name the material, context and period
Vague concepts The role of … / The impact of media on society Name the phenomenon itself: “Representation of …”, “Readings of …”
Redundancy and filler Differences and similarities “A Comparative Analysis”
Causal or evaluative claims in a qualitative study Negative portrayal, harmful effects Interpretive wording: “experiences”, “representation”, “negotiating”
Packing the abstract into the title A 30-word title Around 10–16 words; details go in the abstract
Stacked method terms A qualitative descriptive analytical field study One design term is usually enough

Note: feedback, formulations and suggested scores come from transparent programmed rules, not artificial intelligence. The tool supports reflection and does not replace academic supervision.

Frequently asked questions and troubleshooting

Why can’t I move to the next step?
Some required fields (marked with an asterisk) are empty or too short. A message appears at the top of the step and each missing field is highlighted with a note below it; complete them and click Next again.
Where are my answers saved?
In this browser’s local storage on this device only; nothing is sent to a server. On another device or browser your answers appear only after you import a JSON file you exported earlier.
Are the formulations generated in Step 4 final titles?
No. They are rule-based templates to help you start, and you should always edit them so they reflect your study accurately.
What is the difference between “Title Quality Evaluation” in the menu and Step 6?
Step 6 is a five-point rubric with ten criteria. The Title Quality Evaluation section is a fifteen-item checklist you can use with any title, even outside the Builder.
How do I save the report as a PDF?
In the final step click the print button (“Print” or “Print / save as PDF”), then choose “Save as PDF” in your browser’s print dialog. Only the report is printed, not the rest of the site.
Are my answers translated when I switch to the Arabic version?
No. Your answers move to the other version exactly as you wrote them; only the interface and guidance change language.
My answers have disappeared. What happened?
Usually the browser data was cleared, private (incognito) browsing was used, or the tool was opened in a different browser. They can only be restored from an exported JSON file, so export a backup regularly.
Does the tool use artificial intelligence?
No. All feedback, formulations and suggested scores come from transparent programmed rules (word lists and templates), so the tool may miss a problem or flag something that is fine; your judgement and your supervisor’s come first.
