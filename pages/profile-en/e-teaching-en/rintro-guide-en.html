User Guide: Research Introduction Writing and Refinement

This tool takes you in thirteen steps from a research idea or a completed study to a coherent, evidence-supported research introduction: context, scholarly background, gap or critical problem, research problem, rationale, aims and questions, theoretical positioning and contribution, then construction, refinement, evaluation and a final report.

Overview

Introduction to the tool: the Research Introduction Builder is a guided writing and refinement laboratory. Each step explains a concept, shows a film or television example with weaker and better formulations, collects your entries and checks them with transparent rules. All steps feed one introduction draft.

Academic purpose and intended users: postgraduate students, doctoral researchers and academics in the Cinema and Television track of the Department of Audio-Visual Production who write introductions for journal articles, theses, conference papers, book chapters or proposals. The tool prioritises qualitative and mixed-methods research (interviews, focus groups, reception studies, textual, semiotic, narrative and discourse analysis) and also supports surveys, quantitative content analysis and critical or theoretical work. It does not impose one rigid structure: an interpretive film analysis may open with a critical puzzle, whereas a survey usually builds on prior evidence and measurable questions.

Main features:

  • A thirteen-step Research Introduction Builder with required-field checks, progress indicators and a “Guidance for this step” link in every step.
  • A Scholarly Context and Citation Matrix (Tool C) with CSV and JSON import and export.
  • A Research Gap and Problem Positioning Tool (Tool B) that requires evidence for every gap and distinguishes four levels of claim.
  • A Research Alignment Checker (Tool D) comparing the problem, gap, aim, objectives, questions and contribution.
  • An Introduction Construction Tool with eight reorderable rhetorical moves and optional subheadings.
  • A rule-based Refinement Assistant (Tool F), a Coherence Checklist (Tool E), a ten-criterion Quality Evaluation (Tool G), an Examples Library (Tool H), a Structure Builder (Tool A) and a Bilingual Terminology Guide (Tool I).
  • A final report you can edit, copy, print, save as PDF or download as Word or text.

The workflow: Step 1 records your profile. Steps 2–9 build the argument: context, background, gap, problem, rationale, aims and questions, theory and contribution. Step 10 assembles the introduction; Step 11 refines it; Step 12 evaluates it; Step 13 produces the final version and report.

Have ready before you start: your topic and design, the key studies you will cite (with full references), a note of how you searched the literature, and your target publication’s guidelines. The tool uses only what you enter: it never invents studies, gaps, references or claims.

Moving around: use Next step and Previous step, or the numbered circles. Fields marked with an asterisk (*) are required; you cannot continue until they are complete. Everything saves automatically on this device.

Example used in this guide: a planned qualitative focus-group study of how young Saudi women choose and interpret Turkish serials on streaming platforms, written as a journal introduction without subheadings.

Accessing the tool

Open the tool from the Teaching Experience page (Interactive Research Tools) or directly at: Research Introduction Writing and Refinement in Film and Television Studies. Every Builder step also has a “Guidance for this step” link that opens this guide at the same step in a new tab.

Learning outcomes

After completing the tool’s steps you will be able to:

  • Establish a broader research context that moves from the field to a focused scholarly issue.
  • Critically synthesise the most relevant previous studies instead of summarising them one by one.
  • Identify an evidence-supported research gap or critical problem at the right level of claim.
  • Formulate a scholarly research problem, rationale and significance without exaggeration.
  • Write an aim, objectives and questions aligned with the problem and design, with brief theoretical positioning and a credible contribution.
  • Construct, refine and self-assess a coherent introduction and prepare a researcher-reviewed final report.

Reading the feedback

  • Colours and statuses: “Good” (green) needs no work, “Revise” (amber) is worth a look, “Missing” (red) is an essential element that is absent, and “Note” (blue) is information to consider.
  • Outputs under Steps 1–9: show the statement that will enter the introduction, in your words, with short notes.
  • Gap levels: the check compares the level you chose with your evidence and search basis; a “supported” gap needs a described search and at least two studies.
  • Alignment: shared key terms between problem, aim, question, gap and contribution are prompts, not proof; read the elements together in the Research Alignment section.
  • Moves: ✓ means cue words for the move were found and ? means they were not; it is a prompt to look, not a verdict.
  • Suggested scores: appear under each criterion in Step 12 and come from the checks; accept or change them. Recommendations appear for criteria rated 3 or below.

Guidance on scholarly referencing

  • Cite claims, not decoration: every sentence about what scholars have found, argued or neglected needs an in-text citation. The checker flags phrases such as “studies show” without one.
  • One style, consistently: the tool assumes APA 7 (Author, Year). Use “et al.” for three or more authors and list works alphabetically in the reference list.
  • Full references: enter the complete reference with its DOI in the matrix or in Step 13; check every DOI by opening it.
  • Recent and foundational: prefer recent peer-reviewed studies (for example 2020 onwards) for the current state of research, and cite foundational works (such as Swales, 1990) for established concepts.
  • Do not cite what you have not read: if you know a study only through another source, cite it as a secondary source or find the original.
  • Gap claims need their search: describe databases, terms and years for any “supported” gap.
  • Journal rankings: do not state Q1/Q2 or indexing unless you have checked the current SJR or JCR listing.

Saving, editing, copying, printing and exporting

  • Automatic saving: every change is saved in this browser on this device; the time of the last save appears above the steps.
  • Editing: return to any step through the numbered circles; later steps update automatically unless you edited a move or the full draft directly.
  • Copying: use Copy next to the draft, “Copy final introduction” or “Copy report”.
  • Printing and PDF: in Step 13 click “Print / Save as PDF” and choose “Save as PDF”. Only the report is printed.
  • Downloading: “Download Word (.doc)” and “Download text (.txt)”.
  • Matrix files: export the citation matrix as CSV (opens in Excel or Google Sheets) or JSON; import adds rows to the existing matrix.
  • Project files: “Export my project (JSON)” saves everything; “Import project” restores it on another device or browser.
  • Switching language: your entries and current step are kept; your own text is never translated.
  • Deleting: “Delete my saved data” (click twice to confirm) removes your project from this browser. Do this on shared computers.

Troubleshooting

  • “Next step” does nothing: a required field is empty or too short; the message at the top and the highlighted fields show what is missing.
  • The draft did not change after I edited an earlier step: you edited the move or the full draft directly. Use “Reset” on the move or “Rebuild from moves”.
  • A move is missing from the draft: the move is switched off, or the related step is empty (for example Step 8 with “No formal theory”).
  • The CSV import failed: the first row must contain the column names (cite, ref, claim…) or the labels shown in the matrix; save the file as CSV (UTF-8).
  • Arabic text looks wrong in Excel: open the CSV through Data › From Text/CSV and choose UTF-8.
  • My data disappeared: data are stored per browser and device; private windows and clearing site data remove them. Import your exported JSON file.
  • The printed report includes other parts of the page: print from Step 13 using the button, not the browser menu, and refresh if the page was open for a long time.
  • Subheadings cannot be switched on: Step 1 is set to “No subheadings”.

Academic integrity and limitations

  • Your content, your responsibility: every statement in the introduction comes from what you entered. You remain responsible for its accuracy and for every citation.
  • No invented content: the tool does not generate studies, gaps, findings, references or claims. Templates only arrange your words; illustrative examples are labelled and are not real studies.
  • Rule-based, not AI: checks rely on word lists, counts, citation patterns and term overlap. They can miss problems or flag acceptable wording, and they do not evaluate meaning or scholarly quality.
  • Gap claims: the tool cannot verify that a gap exists; it only checks that your wording matches the evidence you report.
  • Translation: the tool does not translate. For two languages, write each version yourself.
  • Not a substitute: the tool supports learning and refinement. It does not replace supervision, source verification or peer review.

Frequently asked questions

Why can’t I move to the next step?
Some required fields (marked with an asterisk) are empty or too short. A message appears at the top of the step and each missing field is highlighted with a note below it; complete them and click Next again.
Where are my answers saved?
In this browser’s local storage on this device only; nothing is sent to a server. On another device or browser your answers appear only after you import a JSON file you exported earlier.
Why didn’t the draft change after I edited an earlier step?
Because you edited the move or the full draft directly, so automatic updating stopped to protect your edits. Use “Reset” for a move or “Rebuild from moves” for the draft.
Does the tool write my introduction or find studies for me?
No. Templates only arrange your own words and the checks are rule-based prompts; no studies, gaps, references or claims are added that you did not enter.
When should I choose “supported gap”?
Only when you searched named databases with stated terms, years and languages and can describe what you found. Otherwise choose “potential gap” or “limited attention”, or frame it as a critical problem.
Do I need a named theory?
No. Choose “No formal theory warranted” in Step 8 if your design is exploratory or descriptive, and justify it in one sentence.
How do I move the citation matrix into Excel?
Use “Export CSV” in Step 3 or in the Scholarly Background section and open the file in Excel as UTF-8. To bring it back, edit and save as CSV, then use “Import CSV or JSON”.
How do I save the report as a PDF?
In the final step click the print button (“Print” or “Print / save as PDF”), then choose “Save as PDF” in your browser’s print dialog. Only the report is printed, not the rest of the site.
Are my answers translated when I switch to the Arabic version?
No. Your answers move to the other version exactly as you wrote them; only the interface and guidance change language.
My answers have disappeared. What happened?
Usually the browser data was cleared, private (incognito) browsing was used, or the tool was opened in a different browser. They can only be restored from an exported JSON file, so export a backup regularly.
Does the tool use artificial intelligence?
No. All feedback, formulations and suggested scores come from transparent programmed rules (word lists and templates), so the tool may miss a problem or flag something that is fine; your judgement and your supervisor’s come first.
