Overview

Who it is for: postgraduate students, doctoral researchers and academics in Film and Television Studies preparing an abstract for a journal article, thesis, conference submission or book chapter. It supports qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods studies, including interviews, focus groups, textual, semiotic, narrative and discourse analysis, surveys and content analysis.

Academic purpose: the tool works as a guided writing and refinement laboratory, not a list of tips. It helps you identify the essential parts of an abstract, extract the key information from your finished study, choose a structure suited to your method and publication, write concise statements of purpose, method, findings and contribution, combine them into one text, and refine it for clarity, precision and compliance.

Main features:

  • A twelve-step Research Abstract Builder whose methodology fields adapt to your research design.
  • An Abstract Construction Tool that combines your statements into a structured or unstructured abstract, with sections you can switch on or off, reorder, rename and edit.
  • Transparent refinement checks (long sentences, repetition, vague or overclaiming words, citations, abbreviations, tense, placeholders and the five abstract moves).
  • A word count and compliance tool, a keywords builder, a ten-criterion evaluation rubric, and a final report you can copy, print, save as PDF or download.
  • Tools A–H outside the Builder: structure selector, information extractor, word counter, language checker, refinement checklist, quality rubric, examples library and bilingual terminology guide.

The workflow: steps 1–6 collect your information (profile, background, aim, methodology, findings, contribution); step 7 builds the abstract; steps 8–11 refine and evaluate it (language, word count and rules, keywords, rubric); step 12 produces the final version and report.

Have ready before you start: your completed paper or thesis chapter, the target publication’s author guidelines (word limit, format, keywords), and your actual findings. The tool uses only what you enter: it never invents findings, numbers or claims.

Moving around: use Next step and Previous step, or the numbered circles at the top. Fields marked with an asterisk (*) are required. Your answers save automatically on this device.

Example used in this guide: a qualitative focus-group study of how young Saudi women interpret romance in Turkish television drama, written as a 250-word unstructured journal abstract.

Accessing the tool

Open the tool from the Teaching Experience page (Digital Learning Resources) or directly at: Research Abstract Writing and Refinement in Film and Television Studies. Every Builder step also has a “Guidance for this step” link that opens this guide at the same step in a new tab.

Learning outcomes

After completing the tool’s steps you will be able to:

  • Identify the essential components of a research abstract and its five moves: context, purpose, method, findings and contribution.
  • Extract the most important information from a completed study and express it in concise statements.
  • Choose an abstract structure (structured or unstructured) suited to the method and the publication.
  • Describe the methodology accurately for qualitative, quantitative or mixed designs, and report findings without exaggeration.
  • Refine the language, comply with word limits and publication rules, and select disciplinary keywords.
  • Self-assess the abstract against ten criteria and prepare a researcher-reviewed final version.

Step 2 — Background and context

Field Purpose Required information How to complete it Example Mistake to avoid
Research topic Feeds keyword suggestions (not copied into the abstract) A short phrase Three to six words Gulf audiences of Turkish drama Writing a full paragraph
Scholarly context or phenomenon * Opens the abstract The phenomenon and why it matters One sentence, no citations Turkish television drama is widely watched in the Gulf. A history of cinema since 1895
Central issue Sharpens the context What is changing or contested One short sentence Streaming platforms now offer both broadcast serials and originals. Repeating the context
Research problem or gap * Leads to the aim What is not yet known Specific, ending the background Few studies have examined how young viewers choose between these forms. “Little research exists.”
Justification Shows significance Why answering matters Optional; one sentence Understanding these choices helps explain transnational television flows. Overstating importance

The output shows the background statement and its word count. A warning appears if it takes more than about 30% of your word limit or contains citations.

Step 3 — Aim and objectives

Field Purpose Required information How to complete it Example Mistake to avoid
Main research aim * States the purpose A purpose verb + focus Begin “This study examines / explores / analyses…” This study explores how young Saudi women interpret romance in Turkish dizi. “This research is about Turkish drama.” (a topic, not a purpose)
Specific objectives Checks consistency Detailed objectives Usually not copied into the abstract To compare broadcast and streaming preferences Listing every objective in the abstract
Research question Optional clarity The main question Tick “Include the research question” only if it is short How do participants explain their preference for broadcast dizi? Including several long questions
Research focus Narrows the aim Texts, audience or setting A short phrase university students in Jeddah Repeating the aim

The check confirms a purpose verb and warns if the aim reads like a topic or is longer than about 45 words.

Step 4 — Methodology

Choose the approach and design (they are shared with Step 1). The fields then change to match the design, so irrelevant elements are not forced into the abstract:

Design type Fields shown Required
Interviews, focus groups, reception study, ethnography Participants · Recruitment approach · Interviews or focus groups · Analytical procedure · Context Participants, data collection, analysis
Textual, semiotic, narrative, discourse, qualitative content analysis, comparative case Selected films/series/episodes/scenes · Selection rationale · Analytical framework · Interpretive procedure · Production or cultural context Materials, procedure
Survey or experiment Population and sample · Sampling method · Instrument · Statistical analysis · Context Sample, instrument, analysis
Quantitative content analysis Sample of texts · Sampling · Coding scheme · Analysis and reliability · Context Texts, coding scheme, analysis
Mixed methods Qualitative component · Quantitative component · Integration · Overall participants · Context Both components and integration
Historical, document or review studies Sources · Selection · Guiding framework · Analytical approach Sources, approach
Field Purpose Required information How to complete it Example Mistake to avoid
Participants / sample Shows whose data Number and key characteristics Write as a phrase that fits “with …” 36 university students in Jeddah Percentages for qualitative data
Recruitment / sampling Shows how chosen Method of selection A short phrase purposive and snowball sampling “Random” when it was not
Data collection Names the data Type, number and mode A phrase that fits “through …” six focus groups Describing the interview guide in detail
Selected materials (textual) Defines the corpus Films, series, episodes or scenes Name them precisely 12 episodes of two Saudi streaming dramas “Some films”
Analytical framework Shows the lens Theory or codebook Name it; no citation needed Barthes’s semiology Citing authors with years
Analysis Shows the procedure The technique Use the precise name reflexive thematic analysis “The data were analysed.”
Integration (mixed) Distinguishes mixed methods How strands combine Name the procedure a joint display comparing survey patterns with themes Two parallel studies with no link

The methodology statement is built from your phrases, for example: “The study adopted a qualitative design based on focus groups. Data were collected through six focus groups with 36 university students in Jeddah. The data were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis.” A warning appears if statistical terms appear in a qualitative description.

Step 5 — Findings

Field Purpose Required information How to complete it Example Mistake to avoid
Main finding * The core of the abstract Your most important actual result One or two sentences, stated plainly Participants accepted romance when it remained within family-centred narratives. “Many interesting results were found.”
Supporting finding Adds depth A second result One sentence Social media drove the discovery of new serials. Repeating the main finding
Major themes (qualitative) Interprets the data Themes and what they show Sentences, not a list of codes Viewers framed romance as acceptable within family storylines. “Themes: love, family, religion, fashion, language.”
Key quantitative results Reports evidence Direction, size, significance Values as the journal expects Exposure was associated with viewing AI as a threat (p = .003, Cramér’s V = .14). “Significant” without the statistic
Answer to the question Links findings to the question One sentence Optional but recommended These preferences show that cultural appropriateness shapes platform choice. Introducing new results here

Important: the tool never generates findings. Enter your actual results from the paper.

Step 6 — Contribution and implications

Field Purpose Required information How to complete it Example Mistake to avoid
Main scholarly contribution * States what is new What the study adds One sentence The findings refine cultural proximity theory by showing how proximity is negotiated. “This study is very important.”
Theoretical contribution Links to theory Theory extended or questioned Optional It extends cultural proximity theory to streaming-era reception. Naming a theory you did not use
Methodological contribution Only if relevant New or adapted method Optional It shows how participant-led focus groups capture platform choices. Claiming novelty for a standard method
Contextual contribution Region, period or platform What is added about the context Optional It provides evidence from Gulf audiences. Generalising to “all Arab audiences”
Practical implications For practice Only if supported Optional Platforms may benefit from culturally adapted originals. Recommendations the data cannot support
Limitations Optional in abstracts Key limitation Include only if expected The sample was limited to university students. A long list of limitations

The table in this step separates a finding, an implication and an unsupported generalisation. Words such as “proves”, “the first study”, “all viewers” are flagged.

Reading the feedback

  • Colours and statuses: “Good” (green) needs no work, “Revise” (amber) is worth a look, “Missing” (red) is an essential element that is absent, and “Note” (blue) is information to consider.
  • Outputs under Steps 1–6: show the statement that will enter the abstract, in your words, with short notes on length, purpose or overclaiming.
  • Word meter: the bar turns gold above 90% of the limit and red when the limit is exceeded.
  • Abstract moves: ✓ means cue words for the move were found and ? means they were not; it is a prompt to look, not a verdict.
  • Suggested scores: appear under each criterion in Step 11 and come from the checks; accept or change them. Recommendations appear for criteria rated 3 or below.

Other sections of the website

Tool A — Abstract Structure Selector: answer four questions (guidelines, venue, type of study, word limit) to get a recommendation and the reasons for it. When the guidelines are silent it recommends an informative unstructured paragraph that follows the five moves.

Tool B — Research Information Extractor: nine questions about your finished study. The answers are shared with the Builder, and each has a link to the matching step.

Methodology Summary: what to report for each design type, with a weaker and a stronger example.

Findings and Contribution: guidance for qualitative, quantitative and mixed findings; a table separating findings, implications and generalisations; and hedged and supported reporting language.

Tool C — Abstract Word Counter: paste any text, choose a limit (presets are examples) and see words, characters, sentences and paragraph counts. “Use my Builder draft” copies your draft in.

Tool D — Academic Language Checker: the same rule-based checks as Step 8 for any pasted text, with move detection.

Tool E — Abstract Refinement Checklist: twelve items, shared with Step 8, with a progress count.

Tool F — Abstract Quality Rubric: descriptors for scores 1, 3 and 5 on each criterion, with a link to Step 11.

Tool G — Academic Examples Library: seven illustrative teaching examples (labelled as such) with each move highlighted, covering Turkish series audiences, Saudi cinema, feminist criticism, semiotic analysis, streaming audiences, interviews with creative professionals and mixed-methods audience research; and ten verified published studies with what to learn from their abstracts.

Tool H — Bilingual Academic Terminology Guide: searchable Arabic–English definitions of abstract-writing terms.

Saving, editing, copying, printing and exporting

  • Automatic saving: every change is saved in this browser on this device. The time of the last save appears above the steps.
  • Editing: you can return to any completed step through the numbered circles; later steps update automatically unless you have edited the section or the full draft directly.
  • Copying: use Copy next to the draft, “Copy final version” or “Copy report”.
  • Printing and PDF: in Step 12 click “Print / Save as PDF” and choose “Save as PDF”. Only the report is printed.
  • Downloading: “Download editable (.doc)” and “Download text (.txt)”.
  • Export and import: “Export my project (JSON)” saves everything to a file; “Import project” restores it on another device or browser.
  • Deleting: “Delete my saved data” (click twice to confirm) removes your project from this browser. Do this on shared computers.

Common mistakes

Mistake Why it is a problem What to do
Writing the abstract before the study is finished It may promise results the paper does not deliver Write or revise it last, from the final paper
Long background Findings are squeezed out One or two sentences ending with the gap
Announcing instead of stating findings Readers cannot judge the study State the main finding explicitly
Citations in the abstract Most journals do not allow them Remove them; name theories instead
Statistical language in qualitative work Misrepresents the method Report themes, not percentages or significance
Listing codes Shows data, not findings Say what the themes reveal
Overclaiming and generalising Goes beyond the evidence Use hedged language; specify the group or texts
Different numbers or terms from the paper Damages credibility Check every figure and term
Ignoring the guidelines Delays review Use Step 9 and the compliance summary
Literal translation of keywords Terms may not be searchable Use established disciplinary terms in each language

Academic integrity and limitations

  • Your content, your responsibility: every statement in the abstract comes from what you entered. The researcher remains responsible for its accuracy and its consistency with the full paper.
  • No invented content: the tool does not generate findings, numbers, references or claims. Templates only arrange your own words; illustrative examples are labelled and are not real studies.
  • Rule-based, not AI: checks rely on word lists, counts and cue words. They can miss problems or flag acceptable wording; they do not evaluate meaning or scholarly quality.
  • Translation: the tool does not translate. For bilingual abstracts, write each version yourself and check that specialised terms are used correctly in each language.
  • Requirements vary: word-limit presets and structures are examples; your target publication’s current guidelines always take precedence.
  • Not a substitute: the tool supports learning and refinement. It does not replace supervision, peer review or editorial assessment.

Frequently asked questions and troubleshooting

Why can’t I move to the next step?
Some required fields (marked with an asterisk) are empty or too short. A message appears at the top of the step and each missing field is highlighted with a note below it; complete them and click Next again.
Where are my answers saved?
In this browser’s local storage on this device only; nothing is sent to a server. On another device or browser your answers appear only after you import a JSON file you exported earlier.
Why didn’t the draft change after I edited an earlier step?
Because you edited the full draft or that section directly, so automatic updating stopped to protect your edits. Use “Reset” for a section or “Rebuild from sections” for the draft.
Does the tool write my findings or paraphrase my abstract?
No. Templates only arrange your own words and the checks are rule-based prompts; no findings, numbers or claims are added that you did not enter.
Does the word count match Microsoft Word?
Words are counted as groups of characters separated by spaces; word processors may differ by a few words, especially in Arabic, so leave a small margin.
I need an Arabic and an English abstract. How do I do it?
Choose “Arabic and English” in Step 1, write the first version fully, then switch to the tool in the other language and write the second version yourself; enter keywords in both languages in Step 10.
How do I save the report as a PDF?
In the final step click the print button (“Print” or “Print / save as PDF”), then choose “Save as PDF” in your browser’s print dialog. Only the report is printed, not the rest of the site.
Are my answers translated when I switch to the Arabic version?
No. Your answers move to the other version exactly as you wrote them; only the interface and guidance change language.
My answers have disappeared. What happened?
Usually the browser data was cleared, private (incognito) browsing was used, or the tool was opened in a different browser. They can only be restored from an exported JSON file, so export a backup regularly.
Does the tool use artificial intelligence?
No. All feedback, formulations and suggested scores come from transparent programmed rules (word lists and templates), so the tool may miss a problem or flag something that is fine; your judgement and your supervisor’s come first.
