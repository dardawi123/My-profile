Conceptual foundations

The research problem is the engine that drives the entire study

The research problem is the core issue a study sets out to examine; technically, it identifies the gap the study will fill. It directly shapes the research aims, questions and methodology, so a vague problem produces unfocused and even conflicting aims and questions.

Research problem vs. problem statement

The research problem is the issue itself, together with its consequences and your response to it, and it may span several paragraphs. The problem statement is a concise, explicit summary, usually no longer than one paragraph, that states what the problem is and how the study will address it.

The four elements of a problem statement

  1. Context: what is currently known about the topic.
  2. Issue: what exactly the problem is and what we need to know.
  3. Relevance: why the study is worthwhile and what value its findings hold.
  4. Objectives: what the study hopes to discover or clarify.

Theoretical or applied?

A theoretical problem starts from a shortcoming in a field of knowledge and seeks to extend it, such as rethinking the “male gaze” (Mulvey, 1975; Smelik, 2016). An applied problem seeks a practical solution to an issue within an institution or industry, such as using artificial intelligence in filmmaking education (Daou, 2025).

What distinguishes the problem in qualitative research

  • It explores a single central phenomenon rather than testing a relationship between variables (Creswell & Poth, 2018).
  • Its questions are open-ended, beginning with “how” and “what”, and evolve during the research through a reflective process (Agee, 2009).
  • It aligns with the goals, conceptual framework, methods and validity criteria in an interactive design (Maxwell, 2013).
  • It may be reformulated when unexpected themes emerge, as when the concept of “digital othering” arose during interviews (Berg, 2025).

What distinguishes the problem in mixed methods research

  • It must justify why a single type of data is not sufficient to answer it (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018).
  • It requires a quantitative question, a qualitative question and an integration question that links them (Tashakkori & Creswell, 2007).
  • Example: the Lord of the Rings audience project gathered nearly 25,000 quantitative and qualitative responses to understand what the film meant to its viewers (Barker & Mathijs, 2012).
  • The attached Saudi study on AI-themed films acknowledged that qualitative triangulation would have provided deeper interpretive insight.

Where does your problem lie? Research sites in film and television

Rose (2016) proposes that visual meaning is made at three sites: production, the image/text itself, and audiencing. Our field adds two further sites: practice-based creative research (Batty & Kerrigan, 2018), and industry, policy and education. Identifying the site is the first decision in formulating the problem.

Where do problems come from?

Nine types of research gap, with examples from our field

This classification combines the seven gap types identified by Miles (2017), which closely parallel the framework of Müller-Bloch & Kranz (2015), with the “new context” and “new frontier” scenarios described in the attached file.

A methodological caution: do not stop at gap-spotting Alvesson & Sandberg (2011) argue that most research questions arise from “gap-spotting” (confusion, neglect, application), which reinforces existing literature rather than challenging it. The more influential alternative is problematization: questioning the assumptions on which prevailing theories rest.
